01

The challenge

New programs bring evolving requirements, assessments, and learner needs. Faculty need clear, verified guidance they can turn into action.

BSAIIO and BSAISEC are the current parallel builds, each with its own pathways, Rise shell, and open questions.

The design challenge is creating one shared readiness structure and review process without flattening the differences faculty need to understand.

02

From information to learning experience

Don't design from assumptions.

The reusable Program Fact Sheet places Source / verified by beside each detail. Unresolved information stays flagged rather than entering the training as fact.

03

The design decisions

Standardize

  • Source verification
  • Learning architecture and templates
  • QA and review practices

Keep program-specific

  • Pathways and assessments
  • Learner needs and constraints
  • Scenarios and supporting resources

Academic readiness doesn't automatically equal available capacity.

Readiness design asks faculty to consider a learner's workload, consistency, engagement, and time—not enthusiasm alone.

04

Quality, reuse + what I learned

A concrete QA catch

The authentic BSAIE readiness template lists 16 objective assessments in one lesson and 15 in another. Rather than guessing, it flags the count for verification before publication.

What carries forward

Earlier BSAIE work informed the reusable approach. BSAIIO and BSAISEC now apply shared architecture, source checks, and review practices while keeping their program content distinct.

Launch outcomes aren't available yet. Source accuracy, completed reviews, and faculty usability remain the next evaluation priorities.

That's when this stopped feeling like two courses and started feeling like a readiness system.

01

The problem

Tutoring and coaching work can become fragmented across learner progress, goals, scores, session notes, family communication, planning, and service management. The design problem was not simply “put tutoring online.” It was making those moving pieces easier to understand and act on.

Private beta today

02

Why I built it

The objective was not merely to digitize coaching. The challenge was creating enough structure to make the work repeatable while preserving the personalization that makes coaching useful in the first place.

Core tension

How do I make this repeatable without making the learner experience feel generic?

05

Product architecture + interaction design

Four user roles share learner information, planning, sessions, service workflows, and progress infrastructure. The interface changes by role because the decisions change by role.

The information boundary is part of the interaction design: not every role should see every field simply because the system stores it.

Role principle

Show the information that supports the decision this person has to make next.

Shared system underneath. Focused next action for each person.

One product decision: parent visibility without operational clutter

Starting point
Administrators, tutors, students, and families all need information about the same learner, but they use it for different decisions.
Design constraint
A parent needs a trustworthy picture of progress—not the full session-management and internal-note workflow used by the tutoring team.
Decision
Keep shared learner progress underneath the experience, but give parents a separate read-only view with curated updates and learning-plan context.
Visible result
The authentic Parent screens show scores, a goal, progress over time, plan phases, and an updates area. Tutor and Admin screens retain their own operational views.

Evidence: Explore the authentic Admin, Tutor, Student, and Parent screens. This illustrates an implemented design decision; it is not presented as an undocumented before-and-after test.

06

Visual system

Across the sign-in experience and question bank, consistent hierarchy, dark surfaces, gradient accents, and clear action patterns help dense information stay scannable. The question bank keeps question type, difficulty, answers, and editing controls visible together.

Real-world use

Lumavi is currently an invite-only private-beta platform for tutoring companies. SarmiereEC is identified publicly as an early partner, while broader testimonials are still being gathered.

Evidence available today

The product is live in private beta with real role-based workflows and an early partner. I am not claiming broad adoption, usage metrics, or product outcomes that are not yet established.

“Lumavi has meaningfully changed how we run our tutoring practice — from intake to session notes to parent communication.”SarmiereEC · Early partner, educational consulting

09

What I learned

Personalization ↔ Scalability

Building an actual product made the trade-offs much less theoretical. Reusable structure can create clarity and reduce repeated work, but too much structure can flatten the learner experience. The design work is deciding what deserves a standard and what still needs room for judgment.

01

The problem wasn't a lack of resources.

Students were navigating coursework, resources, pacing, goals, support, and next steps across multiple places. The design problem was helping them understand what mattered next while creating better coordination around their experience.

Design question

How do you give a learner enough structure to create momentum without turning the support system into one more thing to manage?

06

More wasn't better.

D336 became a useful mature example of the shift toward less duplication and better-timed support.

How much support is helpful before the support itself becomes overwhelming?

Retained

The learner phases, clear next steps, and routes to human support.

Simplified

Overlapping tasks, duplicated guidance, and information shown too early.

Why

Student and mentor feedback pointed to access friction, checkbox timing confusion, and assessment-readiness uncertainty.

The shift also made pacing guidance more flexible, progress easier to see, and resources more useful at the moment they were needed.

The best iteration wasn't adding more. It was deciding what could come out.

07

The decision

Design trade-off

Keep expanding the comprehensive experience—or simplify around what students need at the moment of use?

Decision: Simplify. Retain the useful structure, reduce overlap, and bring support closer to the next action.

08

Promising movement. Not proof.

What we observed

Early internal feedback identified students scheduling objective assessments ahead of their end-of-course goals, attending cohorts, using Planners regularly, and asking more strategic questions in meetings. Feedback also surfaced access delays, confusion around checkbox timing and assessment readiness, and the need for more explicit micro-goals.

What we couldn't conclude

Those observations were useful design signals, not causal proof. They did not isolate FLIGHT as the cause or tell us which individual elements were responsible for the behavior we saw.

09

10

What I learned

More support doesn't automatically mean a better experience.

The clearest shift in the work was moving from “what else can we give students?” to “what is actually useful here?” That meant treating subtraction, timing, and the surrounding implementation system as design work too.

I started by designing the tool. I ended up thinking about the system around the tool.
