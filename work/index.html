01

Challenge + people

New programs create a lot of information at once: requirements, outcomes, assessments, learner context, faculty expectations, stakeholders, and launch decisions. Faculty need more than a collection of facts. They need clear information they can turn into action.

  • Faculty / MentorsClear information they can turn into action.
  • LearnersThe people those decisions ultimately affect.
  • Program stakeholdersOwn requirements and launch context.
  • Learning / publishing partnersHelp turn the design into a released experience.
  • QA / connected launch teamsProtect accuracy, consistency, and implementation.

02

My role

I lead the Training Pod's mentor-readiness work for new School of Technology program launches, translating program requirements and source material into structured faculty learning experiences and a repeatable development process.

For BSAIIO and BSAISEC, that includes separate Loop workspaces and Rise shells, section assignments, fact sheets, course blueprints, source gathering, unresolved-question tracking, QA, and coordination across two builds that need consistency without erasing program differences.

What I own

Learning architecture · program research · source validation · templates · training development · standards · QA · cross-functional coordination · implementation

Program content, publishing, review, and approval remain shared work with the appropriate partners.

Don't design from assumptions.

The Program Fact Sheet comes first. Program information is researched, sourced, verified, and marked unknown when it cannot yet be confirmed. That keeps uncertain details from getting copied into multiple lessons and becoming harder to unwind later.

Scenario pattern

Student question: “I had to do an Intro Term, am I behind?”

The learning pattern is not recall-only. Mentors practice how to normalize the concern, reframe what the pathway means, then move forward into purposeful term planning.

Knowledge checks mirror the actual work and connect directly to the lesson objective, so the experience is about making better mentor decisions—not memorizing program trivia.

05

Designing for real learners

Academic readiness doesn't automatically equal available capacity.

Faculty preparation needs to account for the learner who can do the work and still has constraints around work, family, confidence, pacing, financial pressure, or time. Current readiness content separates academic capability from available capacity and looks at performance, consistency, engagement, workload, and time-management evidence—not enthusiasm alone.

06

Standardize vs. customize

What should become a system, and what still needs to stay specific to the program?

Standardize

  • Source verification
  • Learning architecture
  • Shared quality standards
  • QA and review practices
  • Reusable learning patterns
  • Path to publication

Customize

  • Program details and pathways
  • Assessments and credentials
  • Challenging courses and sequences
  • Learner considerations
  • Career outcomes
  • Program-specific scenarios

A concrete QA catch

The reusable fact-sheet example flags a conflicting objective-assessment count—one lesson says 16 while another says 15—and tells the builder to confirm the final number instead of carrying the inconsistency into the training.

That's the point of the source-of-truth step: uncertainty is visible early enough to resolve.

08

Earlier launches → reusable system

Earlier BSAIE experience

Established useful content, patterns, and launch lessons.

BSAIIO / BSAISEC model

Separate fact sheets, blueprints, workspaces, and builds use the shared pattern without copying program-specific content.

The part I wanted to stop rebuilding from scratch.

Reusable readiness system

Mentor-readiness framework · source-verification pattern · learning architecture · design and accessibility standards · QA conventions · publication workflow

09

Current application

The same readiness framework is now being applied across the BSAIIO and BSAISEC mentor-readiness builds. The shared process stays consistent; the program details, pathways, assessments, learner considerations, and scenarios stay specific.

What I can say today

The framework is in active use across both builds. I am not presenting launch outcomes that are not available yet.

10

What I learned

The part I became most interested in wasn't just building the training. It was figuring out which parts of the process shouldn't have to be reinvented next time.

Building BSAIIO and BSAISEC at the same time made the difference really clear. Some things should be repeatable. Other things have to stay specific to the program and the learners it serves.

That's when this stopped feeling like two courses and started feeling like a readiness system.

01

The problem

Tutoring and coaching work can become fragmented across learner progress, goals, scores, session notes, family communication, planning, and service management. The design problem was not simply “put tutoring online.” It was making those moving pieces easier to understand and act on.

Private beta today

AI study plans · score trajectory · parent transparency · structured session notes · consultation intake · billing summaries

02

Why I built it

The objective was not merely to digitize coaching. The challenge was creating enough structure to make the work repeatable while preserving the personalization that makes coaching useful in the first place.

Core tension

How do I make this repeatable without making the learner experience feel generic?

05

Product architecture

Four user roles sit on top of shared learner information, workflows, service structures, and progress/status infrastructure. The experience changes by role because the decisions change by role.

Shared product layers

User roles · learner information · planning · session workflows · service management · progress/status infrastructure · family visibility

06

Interaction design

Admins need visibility across students, sessions, and scores. Tutors need the information required before, during, and after sessions. Students need progress, goals, practice, and a clear next step. Parents need curated progress visibility and family updates.

The information boundary is part of the interaction design: not every role should see every field simply because the system stores it.

Role principle

Show the information that supports the decision this person has to make next.

07

Visual system

The interface uses consistent hierarchy, dark surfaces, gradient accents, status treatments, and clear next-action patterns so dense information stays scannable.

08

What is in the beta

Planning + progress

AI study plans, goals, score trajectory, practice, and next steps.

Session continuity

Structured session notes with AI polish and context that can carry forward.

Operations

Consultation intake, role-based dashboards, service workflows, and billing summaries.

Family visibility

Parent transparency through curated updates rather than raw internal notes.

I am intentionally not presenting unreleased or deferred features as live without a documented status.

09

Real-world use

Lumavi is currently an invite-only private-beta platform for tutoring companies. SarmiereEC is identified publicly as an early partner, while broader testimonials are still being gathered.

Evidence available today

The product is live in private beta with real role-based workflows and an early partner. I am not claiming broad adoption, usage metrics, or product outcomes that are not yet established.

10

What I learned

Personalization ↔ Scalability

Building an actual product made the trade-offs much less theoretical. Reusable structure can create clarity and reduce repeated work, but too much structure can flatten the learner experience. The design work is deciding what deserves a standard and what still needs room for judgment.

01

The problem wasn't a lack of resources.

Students were navigating coursework, resources, pacing, goals, support, and next steps across multiple places. The design problem was helping them understand what mattered next while creating better coordination around their experience.

Design question

How do you give a learner enough structure to create momentum without turning the support system into one more thing to manage?

04

My role

I created the original FLIGHT Tool student experience and core Planner framework, then helped lead its evolution from concept and pilot into a broader cross-functional student-success initiative.

As the initiative grew, my work expanded into project management, Planner and Loop maintenance, champion communication and support, resource review, stakeholder coordination, revision and dependency tracking, leadership updates, training, workflows, templates, and organizing work across project phases.

Shared work

SharePoint development, automation, technical implementation, instructor content, QA, and broader rollout were collaborative. The case study separates the student experience I created from the larger system the team built around it.

05

The Hub supported the Planner.

After the Planner was established, the Hub and surrounding guidance grew around it to make setup, weekly use, resources, and feedback easier to navigate.

06

FLIGHT Planner

The core individualized student experience.

Step-by-step guidance and centralized resources.

Faculty Hub

Course SharePoint integration

Mentor + instructor support

The human layer that helps students use the Planner as a living support tool instead of a static checklist.

The Planner was the product. The surrounding guidance, people, and processes made it usable.

07

More wasn't better.

D336 became a useful mature example of the shift toward less duplication and better-timed support.

How much support is helpful before the support itself becomes overwhelming?

Earlier

Comprehensive experiences gathered phases, resources, goals, motivation, and support touchpoints into the Planner.

Later

Feedback pushed the design toward simpler navigation, fewer overlapping items, better timing, and clearer progress.

  • Reduced duplicate and overlapping content
  • Surfaced resources closer to when students needed them
  • Simplified navigation
  • Used more flexible pacing and activity-time guidance
  • Made progress easier to see
  • Integrated support more intentionally
  • Improved consistency

The best iteration wasn't adding more. It was deciding what could come out.

08

The decision

Trade-off

Keep expanding the comprehensive experience, or simplify around what students need most at the moment of use?

Option A
Continue expanding the comprehensive experience.
Option B
Simplify around what students needed most at the moment of use.
Decision
Simplify. Keep the useful structure, reduce overlap, and bring support closer to the point where it is needed.

09

Promising movement. Not proof.

What we observed

Early internal feedback identified students scheduling objective assessments ahead of their end-of-course goals, attending cohorts, using Planners regularly, and asking more strategic questions in meetings. Feedback also surfaced access delays, confusion around checkbox timing and assessment readiness, and the need for more explicit micro-goals.

What we couldn't conclude

Those observations were useful design signals, not causal proof. They did not isolate FLIGHT as the cause or tell us which individual elements were responsible for the behavior we saw.

10

The product wasn't the only thing that had to scale.

  1. Student need

  2. Template

  3. Course expertise

  4. Experience build

  5. Review

  6. QA

  7. Launch

  8. Feedback

My role evolved from designing the student product into helping design the repeatable work around it: templates, workflows, training, champion coordination, stakeholder communication, review, and cross-project tracking. I did not personally perform every step in the system.

Current status

FLIGHT remains active for students already receiving it, while additional expansion and future direction are currently paused pending leadership guidance.

11

What I learned

More support doesn't automatically mean a better experience.

The clearest shift in the work was moving from “what else can we give students?” to “what is actually useful here?” That meant treating subtraction, timing, and the surrounding implementation system as design work too.

I started by designing the tool. I ended up thinking about the system around the tool.
